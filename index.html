Events, sample spaces & the three axioms

Probability is just careful bookkeeping about what could happen. Start with three tiny ideas and everything else — Bayes' theorem, expectations, distributions — falls out of them.

The vocabulary

  • Sample space S — the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
  • Event — any subset of S. "The die lands on an even number" is the event {2, 4, 6}.
  • Probability — a rule that assigns each event a number between 0 and 1.
The three axioms of probability
  1. P(A) ≥ 0 — no negative probabilities
  2. P(S) = 1 — something always happens
  3. P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) — when A and B can't both occur

Everything else in this course is derived from these three rules.

Try it: sample spaces

Probability = (favourable outcomes) ÷ (all outcomes), when every outcome is equally likely.

💡
Intuition. A probability is a long-run frequency: if you repeated the experiment forever, the fraction of times the event occurs approaches P(A). That is why P = 0.5 for a fair coin — half of all flips land heads.

Conditional probability & independence

P(A | B) reads "the probability of A given that B happened". Conditioning means we shrink the sample space down to only the worlds where B is true.

Try it: a deck of cards

Conditioning on "face card" throws away 40 of the 52 cards. Only 12 remain, 4 of which are kings.

Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A and B)P(B)
  • P(A | B) probability of A in the world where B is known to be true
  • P(A and B) probability that both happen (the overlap)
  • P(B) probability of the evidence — it must be > 0
Worked example — King given Face card
P(King | Face) = 4/5212/52 = 412 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333

Independence

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Coin flips are independent. Drawing cards without replacement is not — the second draw depends on the first.

P(A | B) = P(A)   ⟺   P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

PMF vs PDF — discrete vs continuous

This is the single most common point of confusion for beginners. Discrete variables have mass you can read off directly; continuous variables have density that you must integrate. Density is not probability.

Discrete

Probability Mass Function (PMF)

P(X = k)

  • Each outcome has a specific probability.
  • Bar heights are probabilities.
  • All bars sum to exactly 1.
  • Example: number of heads in 3 flips.
Continuous

Probability Density Function (PDF)

f(x),   P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx

  • Height at a single point is a density, not a probability.
  • f(x) may exceed 1.
  • Probability = area under the curve.
  • Total area over (−∞, ∞) equals 1.
⚠️
Why this matters in ML. A classifier's output is a PMF over discrete classes. A VAE's latent space is continuous and described by a PDF. Mixing the two up leads to "probability > 1" bugs and wrong loss functions.

The five distributions you'll meet everywhere

Each distribution is a shape of uncertainty with a story. Learn the story and the formula becomes obvious. Use the explorer below to move the parameters and watch the shape respond.

Distribution explorer

Bernoulli

One trial, two outcomes. The coin of machine learning.

P(X = 1) = p
P(X = 0) = 1 − p
Mean = p  ·  Var = p(1 − p)

ML use: binary classification, dropout masks, click-through.

Categorical

One trial, k outcomes. A weighted k-sided die.

P(X = i) = pi,   Σ pi = 1
e.g. cat 0.7, dog 0.2, bird 0.1

ML use: multi-class softmax output, next-token prediction.

Uniform

All outcomes equally likely. Maximum ignorance.

P(X = k) = 1/n
f(x) = 1/(b − a) on [a, b]

ML use: random weight initialisation, random search.

Normal (Gaussian)

The bell curve. Symmetric, peaked, ubiquitous.

f(x) = 1√(2πσ²) · exp(z)
z = −(x − μ)² / (2σ²)  ·  68% · 95% · 99.7%

ML use: weight init, gradient noise, VAE latents.

Poisson

Counts of rare events in a fixed window of time or space.

P(X = k) = λk e−λk!
Mean = Var = λ

ML use: event rates, request counts, arrival processes.

Reading the normal formula, symbol by symbol

f(x) = 1√(2πσ²)  ·  exp( − (x − μ)²2σ² )
  • x — the value you're evaluating the curve at
  • μ (mu) — the mean: the centre of the bell
  • σ (sigma) — the standard deviation: the width of the bell
  • σ² — the variance
  • exp(·) — the exponential function, e raised to the power
  • 1/√(2πσ²) — the normaliser that forces total area = 1

The exponent −(x − μ)² / (2σ²) is always ≤ 0, so the curve never goes negative, and it shrinks fast as x moves away from the mean — that's the bell shape.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For a standard normal, this is where almost all the probability lives.

Expected value & variance

The expected value is the "balance point" of a distribution. The variance is how far the outcomes tend to scatter around it. Together they summarise a whole distribution with two numbers.

Expected value — the probability-weighted average
Discrete:   E[X] = Σ xi · P(X = xi)
Continuous:   E[X] = ∫ x · f(x) dx
  • xi — each possible outcome
  • P(X = xi) — how likely that outcome is (its weight)

A fair die: E[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5. No roll ever gives 3.5 — it's the long-run average.

Variance — the expected squared deviation
Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] = E[X²] − (E[X])²
Standard deviation = √Var(X)
  • E[(X − μ)²] — average squared distance from the mean
  • E[X²] − (E[X])² — the computationally convenient form
  • Squaring keeps negative and positive deviations from cancelling

Interactive: mean & spread

Click a bar to change its probability. Watch the balance point and the spread react.

The vertical line is E[X]; the shaded band is ±1 standard deviation.

💡
In ML: a loss function is an expected value — the average loss over the data distribution. And variance shows up as training stability: high variance in gradients means noisy, jumpy updates.

Joint & marginal distributions

A joint distribution P(X, Y) describes two variables at once. A marginal distribution recovers one variable by summing the other away.

Interactive: weather & umbrellas

Hover any cell to trace its row and column marginals.

Hover a cell to inspect it.

Row totals and column totals are the marginals. The grand total is always 1.

Marginalising — sum out the other variable
P(X = x) = Σy P(X = x, Y = y)
  • P(X, Y) — joint: probability of every combination
  • P(X) — marginal: X's distribution alone
  • Think of it as "collapse the table onto one axis"

Reading the table

X is the weather (0 = sun, 1 = rain); Y is whether someone carries an umbrella. Knowing it's raining makes an umbrella far more likely — so X and Y are not independent.

P(Y = 1 | X = 1) = 0.450.50 = 0.90  ≠  P(Y = 1) = 0.55

Because the conditional differs from the marginal, the variables are dependent.

Why the bell curve shows up everywhere

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT): the sum (or average) of many independent random variables converges to a normal distribution — no matter what you started with.

Interactive: roll dice and watch the bell curve appear

Each "trial" averages n dice. Histogram the averages and see the shape evolve.

What the demo shows

  • 1 die — a flat uniform distribution.
  • 2 dice — triangular: 7 is much more likely than 2 or 12.
  • 30 dice — a near-perfect bell curve.
  • Any starting distribution — try it: the result is still normal.

Why AI people care

  • Measurement errors are ~normal (many small independent sources).
  • Weight initialisation uses normal distributions.
  • SGD gradient noise is ~normal (a sum of many sample gradients).
  • The normal is the maximum-entropy distribution for a fixed mean and variance — the least-biased guess.

Log probabilities — why we never multiply raw probabilities

Multiplying many small numbers together underflows to zero. Taking logs turns products into sums and keeps everything finite. This one trick is why NLP code is full of log_softmax.

The underflow problem
P(sentence) = P(w₁) · P(w₂) · … · P(wn) → 0.0
log P(sentence) = log P(w₁) + log P(w₂) + … + log P(wn)
  • log(a · b) = log(a) + log(b) — multiplication becomes addition
  • Log probabilities are always ≤ 0 (because 0 < P ≤ 1)
  • More negative = less likely
  • Cross-entropy loss = negative log probability of the correct class

50 words, each with probability p.

The product collapses to 0 (or denormal floats) while the log-sum stays perfectly usable.

Softmax, log-softmax & cross-entropy

Neural networks output raw scores called logits. Softmax turns them into a valid probability distribution. Cross-entropy then measures how far that distribution is from the truth.

Softmax
softmax(zi) = exp(zi)Σj exp(zj)
  • All outputs are in (0, 1)
  • All outputs sum to 1 — a valid distribution
  • Preserves the relative ordering of the logits
  • exp() amplifies differences between logits

Softmax playground

Drag the logits. Watch the probabilities, and try the "unsafe" version to see the overflow.

The numerical stability trick

exp(102) overflows a float. But softmax is invariant to adding a constant to every logit — so subtract the max first. Same answer, no overflow.

Stable softmax
z′ = z − max(z)   →   softmax(z) = softmax(z′)
z = [100, 101, 102] → z′ = [−2, −1, 0] → exp(0) = 1 (safe)

Log-softmax

Combines softmax and log in one stable step. PyTorch uses this internally for cross-entropy loss.

log_softmax(zi) = zi − log Σj exp(zj)
computed as zi − max − log Σ exp(zj − max)

Cross-entropy loss

Cross-entropy measures how different two distributions are. Lower is better. When the true distribution puts all mass on one class, it collapses to the negative log-likelihood.

H(p, q) = − Σi pi log qi
single label:   L = −log qtarget
  • p — the true distribution (one-hot for a labelled example)
  • q — the model's predicted distribution
  • Confidence in the wrong answer is punished harshly (log divergence)
  • Perfect prediction → loss 0

Try it: cross-entropy calculator

Compare with torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss() — it gives exactly this number.

Sampling — turning distributions into output

Sampling means drawing random values from a distribution. It is how every generative model actually produces something.

Where sampling shows up in ML

  • Dropout — randomly zeroes neurons during training.
  • Data augmentation — samples random transformations.
  • Language models — sample the next token from the predicted distribution.
  • Diffusion models — sample noise, then progressively denoise it.

Three techniques worth knowing by name

  • Inverse transform sampling — feed a uniform random number through the inverse CDF.
  • Rejection sampling — propose, then accept/reject with some probability.
  • Reparameterisation trick — used by VAEs so gradients can flow through random nodes.

Everything above, in Python

You now understand the ideas. Here is the code — written from scratch, no libraries, so you can see exactly what scipy is doing for you later. 

import math
import random

def factorial(n):
    result = 1
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        result *= i
    return result

def combinations(n, k):
    return factorial(n) // (factorial(k) * factorial(n - k))

def conditional_probability(p_a_and_b, p_b):
    return p_a_and_b / p_b

p_king_given_face = conditional_probability(4/52, 12/52)
print(f"P(King | Face card) = {p_king_given_face:.4f}")   # 0.3333

Read it: combinations(5, 2) = 10 — ten ways to pick 2 items from 5. The factorial formula is "n choose k".

def bernoulli_pmf(k, p):
    return p if k == 1 else (1 - p)

def categorical_pmf(k, probs):
    return probs[k]

def poisson_pmf(k, lam):
    return (lam ** k) * math.exp(-lam) / factorial(k)

def uniform_pdf(x, a, b):
    if a <= x <= b:
        return 1.0 / (b - a)
    return 0.0

def normal_pdf(x, mu, sigma):
    coeff = 1.0 / (sigma * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))
    exponent = -0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2
    return coeff * math.exp(exponent)

Read it: each function is the formula from Lesson 4, typed out. PMFs return probabilities; PDFs return densities.

def expected_value(values, probabilities):
    return sum(v * p for v, p in zip(values, probabilities))

def variance(values, probabilities):
    mu = expected_value(values, probabilities)
    return sum(p * (v - mu) ** 2 for v, p in zip(values, probabilities))

die_values = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]
die_probs = [1/6] * 6
mu = expected_value(die_values, die_probs)
var = variance(die_values, die_probs)
print(f"Die: E[X] = {mu:.4f}, Var(X) = {var:.4f}, SD = {var**0.5:.4f}")
# Die: E[X] = 3.5000, Var(X) = 2.9167, SD = 1.7078

Read it: zip pairs each outcome with its weight. The variance line is literally "average squared distance from the mean".

def sample_bernoulli(p, n=1):
    return [1 if random.random() < p else 0 for _ in range(n)]

def sample_categorical(probs, n=1):
    cumulative = []
    total = 0
    for p in probs:
        total += p
        cumulative.append(total)
    samples = []
    for _ in range(n):
        r = random.random()
        for i, c in enumerate(cumulative):
            if r <= c:
                samples.append(i)
                break
    return samples

def sample_normal_box_muller(mu, sigma, n=1):
    samples = []
    for _ in range(n):
        u1 = random.random()
        u2 = random.random()
        z = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u1)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * u2)
        samples.append(mu + sigma * z)
    return samples

Read it: categorical sampling walks a cumulative total — the same trick used by torch.multinomial. Box–Muller converts two uniforms into one normal.

def softmax(logits):
    max_logit = max(logits)                      # <- stability trick
    shifted = [z - max_logit for z in logits]
    exps = [math.exp(z) for z in shifted]
    total = sum(exps)
    return [e / total for e in exps]

def log_softmax(logits):
    max_logit = max(logits)
    shifted = [z - max_logit for z in logits]
    log_sum_exp = max_logit + math.log(sum(math.exp(z) for z in shifted))
    return [z - log_sum_exp for z in logits]

def cross_entropy_loss(logits, target_index):
    log_probs = log_softmax(logits)
    return -log_probs[target_index]

Read it: cross_entropy_loss is exactly −log q[target] — the negative log-likelihood from Lesson 9.

def demonstrate_clt(dist_fn, n_samples, n_averages):
    """Average `n_samples` draws, repeated `n_averages` times."""
    averages = []
    for _ in range(n_averages):
        samples = [dist_fn() for _ in range(n_samples)]
        averages.append(sum(samples) / len(samples))
    return averages

# Roll one die at a time vs average 30 dice:
single = demonstrate_clt(lambda: random.randint(1, 6), 1, 5000)
many   = demonstrate_clt(lambda: random.randint(1, 6), 30, 5000)
# `many` looks like a bell curve; `single` is flat.

Read it: this is the whole CLT. Change dist_fn to anything — dice, exponentials, coin flips — the averages still go normal.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
xs = [mu + sigma * (i - 500) / 100 for i in range(1001)]

Implement inverse transform sampling for the exponential distribution. Verify by sampling 10,000 values and comparing the histogram to the true PDF.

Hint

For the exponential with rate λ, the CDF is F(x) = 1 − e−λx. Set u = F(x), solve for x, and plug in a uniform u ~ U(0,1): x = −log(1 − u)/λ.

02 Joint tables for two loaded dice

Hint

If independent, every cell must satisfy P(X = i, Y = j) = P(X = i) · P(Y = j). Marginals come from row and column sums. Find one cell where the product rule fails and you have your answer.

Key terms, decoded

What people say versus what the term actually means. Filter to find what you need.

Sample space

"All the possibilities"

The set S of every possible outcome of an experiment.

PMF

"The probability function"

Gives the exact probability of each discrete outcome. All values sum to 1.

PDF

"The probability curve"

A density function for continuous variables. Integrate it over an interval to get probability.

Conditional probability

"Probability given something"

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). The foundation of Bayesian thinking.

Independence

"They don't affect each other"

P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B). Knowing one event tells you nothing about the other.

Expected value

"The average"

The probability-weighted sum of all outcomes. A loss function is an expected value.

Variance

"How spread out"

The expected squared deviation from the mean. High variance = noisy, unstable estimates.

Normal distribution

"The bell curve"

f(x) = (1/√(2πσ²)) · exp(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)). Appears everywhere because of the CLT.

Central Limit Theorem

"Averages become normal"

The mean of many independent samples converges to a normal distribution regardless of the source.

Joint distribution

"Two variables together"

P(X, Y) — the probability of every combination of X and Y outcomes.

Marginal distribution

"Sum out the other variable"

P(X) = Σy P(X, Y). Recovers one variable's distribution from the joint.

Log probability

"Log of the probability"

log P(x). Turns products into sums, preventing numerical underflow.

Softmax

"Turn scores into probabilities"

softmax(zi) = exp(zi) / Σ exp(zj). Maps logits to a valid distribution.

Cross-entropy

"The loss function"

−Σ ptrue log ppred. Measures how different two distributions are. Lower is better.

Logits

"Raw model outputs"

Unnormalized scores before softmax. Named after the logistic function.

Sampling

"Drawing random values"

Generating values according to a probability distribution. How models generate output.
